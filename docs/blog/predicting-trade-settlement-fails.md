# Predicting Trade Settlement Fails: What I’d Do Differently, From SVM + SMOTE to XGBoost + SHAP

*I rebuilt a model I first made at my workplace, this time on 3.1 million synthetic trades where I knew the right answers.*

---

At my workplace, I once built a model to spot trades likely to fail: an SVM trained with SMOTE, a popular trick when the thing you're looking for is rare. I rebuilt it with today's tools to see what I'd change.

**Every trade here is synthetic**, generated by code. That's an advantage: I wrote the rules that decide which trades fail, so I could check whether each model actually found them.

What I'd change:

1. **Class weights** instead of SMOTE's fake trades
2. **XGBoost** instead of an SVM
3. **Calibrated probabilities**
4. **SHAP explanations** for every alert
5. **Daily alerts set by team capacity**, not a fixed cutoff
6. **Leakage tests** before trusting any result

## The problem

When you buy shares, the shares and cash swap hands a day or two later, on the settlement date. If either side doesn't arrive, the trade **fails**: money gets stuck, penalties pile up, and an operations ("ops") team has to chase it. Ops can only check a few trades a day, so the real question is **"which trades should we check first?"**

Accuracy is useless here. 95.5% of test trades settled, so a model that says "nothing will fail" scores 95.5% and catches nothing. Instead I used:

- **PR-AUC:** how well real fails are packed at the top of the risk list (0 to 1; a random list scores 0.045)
- **Fails caught in the riskiest 2%** of trades, roughly what ops can check in a day
- **Brier score:** whether a "30% risk" really fails about 30% of the time (lower is better)

## The data

- **3.1 million trades** over 24 months, and about **3% fail**
- 20 features, like: do the account details match, did the other side confirm quickly, do we have the shares or cash we owe, is it cross-border
- A hidden "recipe" sets each trade's chance of failing, including three combinations that are extra risky together, like wrong account details **and** cross-border
- Real-life mess on purpose: hidden risk, noise, near misses, and some fails with no visible cause

## Three ways to cheat by accident

Data leakage is when the model sees information it won't have in real life, like a student who has seen the exam answers.

1. **Peeking into the future.** "Counterparty fail rate, last 30 days" must only count trades that have already settled.
2. **Shuffled splits.** I split by date instead: 16 months to train, 4 to validate, 4 to test, with a 5-day gap between them.
3. **SMOTE before the split.** SMOTE makes fake fails on the line between two similar real fails.

![How SMOTE makes fake fails](images/fig1_smote_how.png)
*Illustration.*

If SMOTE runs before the split, some fake fails are built from test trades, and the model trains on near copies of the test set. The typical test fail then had a fake fail **about 30 times closer** to it.

![SMOTE before the split puts fake fails right next to test fails](images/fig2_smote_leak.png)
*Synthetic data.*

The fix: make SMOTE a step inside the pipeline, so it only ever sees training data.

```python
from imblearn.pipeline import Pipeline  # imblearn's, not sklearn's

pipe = Pipeline([
    ("encode", encoder),
    ("smote", SMOTENC(categorical_features=cat_cols)),
    ("scale", scaler),
    ("model", LinearSVC()),
])
pipe.fit(X_train, y_train)  # SMOTE runs on training rows only
```

**What I'd do differently:** turn each of these rules into an automated test.

## From SVM to XGBoost

- **SVM** draws the widest possible "street" between fails and non-fails. It compares trades in pairs, so 10 times the data is about 100 times the work. I had to train it on a 100,000-trade sample on a GPU.
- **XGBoost** builds hundreds of small decision trees, each fixing the mistakes of the ones before, like a team of reviewers. It scales to millions of rows.
- **Logistic regression** is the simple baseline. It's easy to read (mismatched account details multiply the odds of failing by about 8.7), but it can't see combinations on its own.

![Precision-recall curves on the test period](images/fig3_pr_curves.png)
*Synthetic data, test period.*

| Model | Best training setup | PR-AUC | Fails caught in riskiest 2% of trades | Brier score |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Random list | | 0.045 | 2% | |
| Logistic regression | No resampling | 0.305 | 21% | 0.037 |
| Linear SVM | No resampling | 0.296 | 20% | 0.037 |
| RBF SVM | Class weights | 0.319 | 22% | 0.036 |
| **XGBoost** | No resampling | **0.333** | **23%** | **0.035** |

*Test period, synthetic data.*

XGBoost ranked best: a **PR-AUC of 0.333**, about 7 times better than random, catching **23%** of all fails in the riskiest 2% of trades.

**What I'd do differently:** start with XGBoost, and keep logistic regression as the baseline to beat.

## Probabilities you can trust

- An SVM outputs a distance from its street, not a probability. **Calibration** maps scores to real probabilities, so a score of 2 means about 79%.
- Class weights inflate risk too. XGBoost's Brier score went from 0.213 to **0.035** after calibration.
- Fit calibration on validation data only, and recheck it as markets change.

## Explaining every alert with SHAP

When ops see a flagged trade, they ask "why?" **SHAP** splits each trade's score into one piece per feature, like a receipt: plus some for mismatched account details, plus some for cross-border, minus some because the shares are in place.

Because I wrote the recipe, I could check SHAP against the truth:

- Its feature ranking matched the recipe closely (**rank correlation 0.96**)
- It ranked the **three planted combinations #1, #2, and #3** out of 190 pairs

![SHAP importance compared with the true recipe](images/fig4_shap_vs_truth.png)
*Gray: the true recipe. Blue: what the model learned. Synthetic data.*

**What I'd do differently:** ship every alert with its reasons, like "fix the account details".

## Set daily alerts by capacity

A cutoff that flagged 2% of trades in validation flagged **6.8%** in the stressed test period, over three times what ops could handle.

![A fixed cutoff flagged 3 times more trades in the stressed test period](images/fig5_daily_alerts.png)
*Synthetic data.*

**What I'd do differently:** each day, flag the **top k** trades, where k is what the team can actually check.

## Limits

I removed one kind of fail from training at a time, then checked how many of them the model still caught:

| Kind of fail | Caught when in training | Caught when never seen |
|---|---|---|
| Wrong account details | 45% | 27% |
| Short on shares or cash | 35% | 14% |
| Upstream trade failed first | 30% | 25% |

*Test period, synthetic data.*

- The model only partly spots fails it has never seen, so label new problems and retrain.
- The data is synthetic: it shows the method, not real-world performance.
